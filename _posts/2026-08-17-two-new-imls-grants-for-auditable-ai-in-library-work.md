The Institute of Museum and Library Services has awarded Virginia Tech University Libraries two 2026 National Leadership Grants totaling $652,397.
I am a co-Principal Investigator on both projects.
The projects address different library services, but both ask what evidence is needed before AI-generated outputs can become dependable parts of library work.

## Machine-generated metadata as infrastructure

The first project, ["Library-wide, Machine-Generated Metadata as First-Class Structural Components"](https://www.imls.gov/grants/awarded/lg-259840-ols-26), received $473,403.
Bipasha Banerjee is the Principal Investigator, and Jennifer L. Goyne and I are co-Principal Investigators.

Digital libraries increasingly use language models to produce descriptions, classifications, named entities, and other forms of metadata.
Generating these fields for individual objects is only the first problem.
A library also needs to know whether the metadata remains consistent across collections, whether relationships derived from it can be traced to source material, and whether it supports useful forms of discovery.

The project will evaluate machine-generated metadata at collection scale and examine the entities and relationships that emerge across digital collections.
It will also study how those relationships can support cross-collection navigation and other patron-facing forms of interaction.
The planned outputs include practical guidance, reproducible evaluation methods, and a prototype for exploring connected collection materials.

## Auditable relevance screening for evidence synthesis

The second project, ["Auditable AI Workflows for Evidence Synthesis Library Services"](https://www.imls.gov/grants/awarded/lg-259841-ols-26), received $178,994.
Bipasha Banerjee is the Principal Investigator, and C. Cozette Comer and I are co-Principal Investigators.

Evidence synthesis begins by identifying which studies meet a review's inclusion criteria.
Title-and-abstract screening can require substantial expert effort, but an automated exclusion can also remove evidence that should have remained in the review.
An AI-assisted workflow therefore needs to make its decisions inspectable and preserve a clear path for human review.

The planning project will develop and evaluate an auditable workflow for AI-assisted relevance screening.
The work will compare locally served open models across prompts, parameters, and repeated runs, then use feedback from evidence-synthesis library professionals to refine a prototype and instruction manual.
The evaluation will emphasize accuracy, recall, disagreement, and stability rather than treating one model response as a definitive answer.

## A shared requirement for library AI

One project evaluates descriptions generated for digital objects, while the other evaluates relevance judgments generated for research records.
In both settings, aggregate model performance is not enough to support a library service.
The output must retain provenance, expose variation, and allow a person to inspect how it was produced.

My role in both projects centers on research design, evaluation, auditability, reproducibility, and reuse.
This work extends my broader research on machine-usable scientific knowledge by asking when generated representations and judgments are reliable enough to support discovery and evidence-based work.

The IMLS award records provide additional information about [the machine-generated metadata project](https://www.imls.gov/grants/awarded/lg-259840-ols-26) and [the evidence-synthesis project](https://www.imls.gov/grants/awarded/lg-259841-ols-26).
